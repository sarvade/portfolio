## What was at stake

Sellers use these analytics pages to decide where to put their money, so the revenue number on them is the product. The business was moving to a new attribution model that separates a direct sale from the influence content had earlier in the buyer's journey. On top of that, different pages were already showing different totals for the same thing.

Get the migration wrong and sellers in three regions make spending decisions on wrong numbers across a full year of history, and nobody finds out until a seller does.

## What I owned

I was brought in to deliver one table. The team was stretched and the integration date had no buffer, so I took ownership of three core tables instead, including the logic they share, and the registration of all 18 new metrics across three attribution tiers.

The business defined the attribution model. My job was to make it true in the data, in three regions, for twelve months back.

## Decisions that mattered

**Merge the codebases while I was already in the code.** The three regions ran on two separate regional codebases, and they had already drifted. That is exactly how one metric ends up computed two different ways. Merging them mid-migration was more work that month, but it is the only time it is cheap. Now a metric changes once, not twice-and-hopefully-identically.

**Ship the contract before the data.** Backend couldn't build APIs against metrics that didn't exist yet, and the tables were due the same day as joint debugging. Instead of racing that dependency, I broke it: I registered all 18 metrics and added the new fields with empty values up front, so backend built against a real contract while I built the pipelines underneath. A sequential handoff became parallel work.

## What the validation phase caught

- **A filter applied at the wrong scope.** QA flagged a revenue discrepancy, and I traced it to a content-type filter that belonged on one attribution path but was applied globally, so every row on that path was being silently excluded. I confirmed the intended semantics with the warehouse lead before touching it, then shipped a narrow fix.
- **A join that fanned out** and quietly multiplied totals.
- **A distinct count at the wrong grain.**

None of the three would have failed a job. All three would have shipped wrong numbers to sellers. The kind of guard that catches the second one, as an illustrative pattern rather than production code:

```sql
-- Grain guard: enriching a fact with dimensions must not change its row count
select (select count(*) from attributed_base
        where dt = '{{ ds }}') as rows_before_join,
       (select count(*) from attributed_enriched
        where dt = '{{ ds }}') as rows_after_join;
-- the run fails if the two counts differ
```

## Result

The migration launched on the program date. Every video analytics page in all three regions reports revenue on the new model, with 12 months of consistent history behind it, and the complaints about pages disagreeing with each other stopped.
