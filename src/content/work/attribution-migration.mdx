## What was at stake

The analytics products I work on show sellers how much revenue their content drives, and sellers use those numbers to decide where to spend. The business was moving to a new attribution model, one that separates a direct sale from the influence content had earlier in the buyer's journey. On top of that, different pages had been showing different totals for the same thing.

If the migration went wrong, sellers in three regions would be making decisions on wrong numbers, across a full year of history.

## What I owned

Four core tables had to move. I was assigned one. The team was stretched and the integration date had no buffer, so I took three of the four, including the logic they shared.

## Decisions that mattered

**One codebase for every region.** Each region had its own pipeline, and that is exactly how one metric ends up computed two different ways. I merged them so a metric can only be defined once.

**Registered the metrics before the data existed.** I pre-registered all 18 metrics in the metric registry before any data existed. Backend engineers could build their APIs against the definitions while I built the pipelines underneath, so the two streams of work ran in parallel instead of in sequence. That's how the date held.

**Kept validation, even with no buffer.** Every region got row-level diffs against the old output and a full 12-month backfill before sign-off.

<Callout label="The call">
  Cutting validation was the obvious way to make the date. I kept row-level diffs and full backfills for every region, and that discipline is what made the bugs below cheap to find instead of expensive to explain.

## What validation caught before launch

- A revenue discrepancy a reviewer raised. It traced cleanly to a single filter applied on the wrong attribution path.
- A join that fanned out and quietly multiplied totals.
- A distinct count computed at the wrong grain.

All three were fixed before launch.

## Running the backfills

The backfills ran for days. I measured throughput as they went, so the program had real ETAs to plan around instead of guesses.

## Result

Every page in all three regions reports revenue on the new model, with 12 months of consistent history behind it, and the complaints about pages disagreeing with each other stopped.

**Scope, plainly:** I led the data engineering delivery for three of the four tables. I didn't define the attribution model itself.
